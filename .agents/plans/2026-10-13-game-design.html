Tickwright: a co‑op anime sky‑island game where exploiting the world's rules on purpose is how you progress

Technical Minecraft players build farms by abusing spawn rules, item physics and redstone timing. Tickwright makes that the whole game. Every rule is short, visible in the world and deterministic. The story gives the farms something to feed, and fast combat tests what they produce.

  • Core loop. Meet a mob, read its rules with Sight (an in-world debug view), build a farm that exploits them, spend the output, then meet a mob whose rules break your last design.
  • Four exploitable systems. Darkness spawning under a mob cap, items as physical objects, Blink Pearls that keep momentum, and Ley, a redstone-like logic.
  • Multiplayer. Co-op worlds of 1 to 8 players, each run by one authoritative server (a Cloudflare Durable Object) using the same simulation code as the browser. The client is Vite and Three.js on Vercel, with Clerk sign-in.
  • First playable slice. One sky island, building, farmable Gloomlings, item drops, and two or more players in one world at 60 fps on a mid laptop.
  • Main risk. A high 2.5D camera can't see into the dark roofed rooms that farms need, so the slice ships a cutaway view.

In a typical session, your crew's farms have been filling chests while anyone was online. You spend the output on gear and parts, then go fight on a new island, where a mob behaves in a way your current farms can't handle. You come back with a new part and an idea, and rebuild. Farms are the progression and combat is the test. Kills are a small source of loot and give no XP.

The rules are the content: short, visible, deterministic, and meant to be exploited

Every system follows three constraints. Each rule fits in one sentence. Sight, an ability every player has, draws the rules in the world: light numbers on blocks, a mark on each cell saying whether a mob could spawn there and, if not, why, the mob cap, and the signal on every wire. And the simulation is deterministic. It ticks 20 times a second in a fixed, documented order, and its randomness comes from a generator seeded per chunk and tick, so the same build gives the same result every time.

Exploits come from combining rules, so there are no special cases. A current that pushes items also pushes mobs, players and pearls.

One tick is 50 ms. Minecraft's technical players work this order out from decompiled code; here the game tells them.

Spawning: dark cells with headroom, far from players, under the cap

Each tick, every loaded chunk picks one random column and a random height up to that column's top block. A Gloomling appears there if the block below is solid, the two above are air, the light is 0, no player is within 24 blocks, some player is within 128, and the world is under its mob cap.

The exploits players will find: light every cave so only the farm fills the mob cap. Build over the void so no other ground shares the farm's columns. Stand 24 to 128 blocks away so spawns happen and the chunks keep running.

Items: real objects that fall, merge, get pushed and expire

A dropped item is an entity with the same physics as everything else. Matching items within half a block merge into one stack, a player picks them up within 1.5 blocks, and they vanish after 5 minutes.

The exploits players will find: a collector moves only 2.5 items per second, so a fast farm needs parallel collector lines. Currents carry items to a central sorter. A pressure plate fires for any entity, items included, so a farm can count its own output.

Item physics numbers

Gravity 0.04 blocks per tick squared, air drag 2% per tick, ground friction 40% per tick. Merge check every 10 ticks, up to 64 per stack. Pickup delay 10 ticks after dropping. Lifetime 6,000 ticks. A collector pulls from the cell above it, holds 5 stacks and passes 1 item every 8 ticks to the container it faces.

Blink Pearls: thrown teleports that keep momentum and can wait in stasis

A thrown pearl is an entity. When it touches a block or a mob, its owner teleports to that spot and takes 2 damage, wherever the owner is, as long as the pearl's chunk is loaded.

The exploits players will find: a pearl held in an upward current floats forever. Cut the current with Ley and it lands, pulling you home from anywhere: a stasis chamber, and a friend can pull the lever for you. Burst blocks add speed to anything above them, so a chain of them is a pearl cannon for reaching far islands.

Ley: redstone-like logic with signal 0 to 15

Ley wire carries a signal from 0 to 15 that drops by 1 per block. Sources set it, components delay or compare it, and outputs act on the world.

KindParts
SourcesLever; button (10-tick pulse); pressure plate (any entity); light sensor (outputs the light level); reader (outputs how full a container is)
ComponentsRepeater (1 to 4 tick delay, restores 15); inverter; comparator
OutputsLamp (light 15, which stops spawning); door; piston (pushes up to 12 blocks and what stands on them); current block; burst block; Anchor (keeps chunks loaded)

The exploit players will find: lamps are Ley outputs and spawning depends on light, so a clock can switch a farm on and off, or flash it to clear mobs out of the cap.

How Ley stays deterministic and which quirks count as features

Wire changes settle inside the same tick, breadth-first from the source that changed. Repeaters and pistons queue their work, and the queue runs first in, first out at the start of the tick it's due. No update order depends on block position or memory layout, so nothing behaves like Minecraft's location-dependent quirks.

The line between exploit and bug: a combination of documented rules is a feature, never patched, and the first person to build a named one earns a Codex entry. Anything that makes results depend on frame rate, latency or load order is an engine bug, and we fix it.

Strain: the entity budget is a rule players can see

Each world can afford a fixed amount of simulation. More than 24 entities in one block cell take crush damage, and a chunk holding more than 200 entities draws the Null (see Story). This keeps the server inside its budget, and it turns a performance limit into a rule players design around instead of lag they suffer.

Law Shards are the endgame. Story bosses drop them. Placing one in your island's Codex Altar amends one law inside your claim: lower the darkness a mob needs from 0 to 3, double item lifetime, or turn off fall damage. A claim holds three. This is "hack the game" taken literally.

The RPG layer rewards understanding. Your character levels up from Insight, earned by discovering things: reading a new rule, building a named exploit for the first time, beating a boss. Kills give none. Insight buys Arts: Sight upgrades such as seeing spawn attempts as they happen, combat moves, and building tools such as copying a structure as a blueprint. Gear is crafted from farm output, so your level shows what you understand and your gear shows what your farms produce.

Story: the Null is the world's garbage collector, so your farms are both the cure and the cause

The Drift is an archipelago of sky islands run by the Codex, a law engine that ticks twenty times a second. People live by its rules without seeing them. You can see them. That Sight makes you a Tickwright, which the Archive, the order that guards the Codex, treats as a crime. Now the Null, a static that deletes whatever it touches, is eating the outer islands, and the Archive's answer is to pull everyone back to the capital. Your crew's answer is to bend the Codex hard enough to push the Null back.

  1. Sight. Your home island frays at the edges. Pell, a retired Tickwright who lost her Sight, teaches you the first farm. Boss: the Gloom Matron, whose summoned adds are themselves farmable.
  2. Currents. The Lantern Isles. You meet Ren, an Archive agent who patches laws; on her islands your old farm designs stop working. The boss drops your first Law Shard.
  3. Blink. Pearl travel to the far isles, where you learn what the Null is: the Codex's cleanup routine, which deletes whatever costs the most to simulate. The capital hoards the budget, so the outer islands get deleted.
  4. Collection. The Null raids your islands in timed attacks called Tides, and your farms become your defenses. You climb to the capital and rewrite the budget law.

Scenes play in the engine with the chibi models and text boxes, one to three minutes each, skippable. Story progress belongs to the world, so the crew shares it. Quests point at the next rule: "Lanternbacks won't jump down. Find another way to move them."

Fast-paced mechanic, to confirm: Flash Step combat that runs on your machine first

I read "fast spcae mechin" as fast-paced combat and movement. If you meant mechs or ships, question 1 rewrites this section.

The core is a dash with two charges and brief invulnerability, a short combo that cancels into the dash, and a parry that refunds a charge. Late game, a Blink Pearl thrown mid-fight lands you behind an enemy. Combat happens on new islands, in boss arenas and during Tides, where your kill chambers, lamp walls and currents fight alongside you. Your own movement runs on your machine with the shared simulation and the server corrects it, so it responds instantly at 100 ms ping.

Combat numbers dash, input forgiveness, parry window

Dash: 4 blocks, 2 charges, 0.8 s recharge each, invulnerable for the first 0.15 s. Inputs forgive the way Celeste's do: you can still jump for 100 ms after leaving a ledge, and jump, dash and attack presses are buffered for 120 ms. Attacks: a 3-hit light combo and a charged heavy. Parry: block within 150 ms of a hit to stagger the attacker. The server confirms hits on mobs.

  • Shading. Two-band cel shading where shadows shift toward cool purple instead of grey. Blocks get per-corner ambient occlusion, characters a rim light. One screen-space pass draws dark plum outlines on everything, so terrain, mobs and players share one line style.
  • Blocks. Flat palette colors with a slight per-block hue shift. Without image textures, every block matches by construction and the download stays small.
  • Characters and mobs. Built in code from rounded boxes and spheres, with big decal eyes and procedural animation: bob, squash and stretch, limb swing. The Gloomling is an indigo blob with glowing cyan eyes that squashes when it lands.
  • Camera. Perspective with a narrow 30° field of view at about 55° pitch, so it reads like an isometric diorama but keeps the depth cues you need to build. Q and E rotate in 45° steps, the wheel zooms, and you aim with the mouse cursor.
  • Cutaway. Farms live in dark roofed rooms that a high camera can't see into. When you walk under a roof, everything above your head fades out, and a layer key slices the world at any height while you build.

Multiplayer: private co-op worlds, each run by one authoritative server

A world holds 1 to 8 players who join by invite link; the owner can grant build rights. The server runs the real simulation at 20 ticks a second. Your browser predicts your own movement with the same code and draws everyone else 100 ms in the past, smoothed between snapshots. Block edits show at once on your screen and roll back if the server rejects them. Worlds save to storage every 10 seconds and when the last player leaves.

Vercel can't hold long-lived WebSockets, so the game server runs elsewhere. I recommend Cloudflare Durable Objects with PartyServer, because a Durable Object is one addressable, single-threaded room with storage attached, which is the exact shape of an authoritative world server, and it costs nothing while the world is empty.

Tech plan: one simulation package that both the browser and the server run

packages/sim      the rules: blocks, light, spawning, entities
packages/net      binary messages, shared
apps/client       Vite + Three.js renderer, input, HUD
apps/server       Worker + one Durable Object per world

The simulation package avoids Math.random, clocks and trigonometry, because browsers may round those differently; tests replay a seed and inputs and compare state hashes. Chunks are 16-block cubes, greedy-meshed in a Web Worker with light and ambient occlusion baked in, and mobs and items are instanced, so 500 items cost one draw call. The budget is 60 fps on an integrated laptop GPU, under 150 draw calls, no allocations per frame, and a server tick under 10 ms with 8 players and 500 entities.

First playable slice: done means two people can farm together

  1. Open the site, sign in with Clerk (or play as a guest in local dev), and land on a small sky island with a ruin that is a working mini farm, so the loop is visible in the first minute.
  2. Move with WASD and jump with Space. Q and E rotate the camera and the wheel zooms. The cutaway reveals roofed rooms.
  3. Left click breaks and right click places five block types. The other player sees the change.
  4. Gloomlings spawn only where the rules allow, and Sight (Tab) shows light levels and why each cell can or can't spawn.
  5. A dark platform with a drop shaft produces Gloom Essence. Items drop, merge and get picked up.
  6. Two browser windows in the same world see each other move, build and fight.
  7. The world survives a server restart.
  8. Frame time measured on a mid laptop and reported with method and spread; target p95 at or under 16.7 ms.

Out of scope for this slice: story scenes, full combat, pearls, Ley, crafting, touch controls, PvP, offline simulation and any production deploy. Deploying a preview to Vercel is in scope once it runs locally.
